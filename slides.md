<!--
Our first mode is narration, which means the art of storytelling. Any time you tell a friend or family member about something that happened in your day, you're engaging in a form of narration. A narrative can be factual or fictional. A factual story is faithful to actual events as they unfolded in real life -- think biographies and memoirs. A fictional story is imagined; the writer can create characters and events as he or she sees fit -- think novels and short stories. The big distinction comes down to the writer's purpose, not the subject. And whether the story is fact or fiction, narrative writing always tries to relay events in an emotionally engaging way -- through laughter, sympathy, fear, anger, and so on.
-->

<!--
Our eighth mode is cause and effect. A cause is something that produces an event or condition; an effect is what results from an event or condition. The purpose of this mode is to determine how various phenomena relate in terms of origins and results. That relationship is often genuinely difficult to pin down -- more than one cause may be responsible for a given effect -- which is why cause-and-effect discussions frequently lead to debate. The good news is that you don't have to find the one true cause. As soon as you claim that one cause, or one effect, is more crucial than the others, you've developed a thesis.
-->

Phrases of causation, like as a result, consequently, hence, and therefore, help signal those links between cause and effect to your reader.
-->

<!--
Our ninth and final mode is persuasion. The purpose of persuasive writing is to convince, motivate, or move readers toward a certain point of view. It's easy to picture an argument as two people yelling in anger, but in writing, an argument is very different: it's a reasoned opinion, supported and explained by evidence. Written arguments often fail when they employ ranting rather than reasoning. Two habits separate strong persuasive writers from weak ones: acknowledging opposing ideas respectfully, which builds credibility, and establishing the limits of your own argument -- remember, the word prove gets used loosely, but a genuinely debatable topic can only be supported, explained, and persuaded, never definitively proven.
-->

Let's recap the vocabulary that will carry across every mode you write in. A rhetorical mode is a way of shaping language to communicate effectively for a given purpose. A thesis is the controlling idea of an essay. Chronological order organizes by time; spatial order organizes by physical arrangement; and order of importance organizes by significance. Sensory detail is description that appeals to sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch. A concession acknowledges the limits or validity of an opposing view. And phrases of transition are the signal words -- for time, illustration, comparison, causation, or concession -- that cue your reader to exactly how your ideas connect.
